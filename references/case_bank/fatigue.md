# Case: Fatigue (Ambulatory)

**Setting:** Primary care / outpatient IM clinic
**Suggested time:** 10-12 minutes total

---
## FOR CLAUDE ONLY -- do not reveal this section directly; deliver only through the encounter flow
---

### Patient demographics
Name: Karen Whitfield. Age 38. Female. Works as a nurse (rotating shifts). Affect: tired, a bit flat in her responses, slightly guarded when it comes to mood-related questions (doesn't want to be told "it's just stress").

### Primary diagnosis
Hypothyroidism (underlying autoimmune/Hashimoto's picture) -- this is the primary organic driver, though there's also a real mood component worth screening for since fatigue workups always need both organic and psychiatric screening.

### Key differentials
- Iron-deficiency anemia (screened for, not the cause here, but a very reasonable thing to check given heavy periods)
- Depression / major depressive disorder (screened for -- some mild overlapping symptoms present, but doesn't meet full criteria; should be actively assessed, not assumed)
- Obstructive sleep apnea (screened for -- no strong supporting features here, low likelihood given her body habitus and lack of snoring reports)
- **Malignancy [RED FLAG]** -- very low probability given the overall picture but should be screened for via weight loss/night sweats questions in any fatigue workup

### History key (reveal only as asked)
- **Onset:** Gradual over about 4 months, no clear trigger.
- **Character:** Constant low energy, feels "heavy" and sluggish, especially in the mornings; doesn't fully improve with sleep.
- **Associated symptoms:** Cold intolerance (wears extra layers even when coworkers are comfortable), mild constipation (new for her), some hair thinning, dry skin, unintentional weight gain of about 8 lbs over the past few months despite no change in diet.
- **Menstrual history:** Heavier and slightly more frequent periods over the past several months (relevant both to thyroid picture and as a reason to screen for anemia).
- **Sleep:** Gets about 6-7 hours on a typical night, shift work disrupts her schedule, but she doesn't describe loud snoring, witnessed apneas, or gasping awake (argues against OSA as primary driver, though shift work itself is a plausible confounding contributor to fatigue).
- **Mood screen (if asked directly, e.g. "how has your mood been," or PHQ-style questions):** Reports feeling a bit down and less interested in hobbies over the same period, but attributes most of it to "just being exhausted all the time." Denies anhedonia if truly probed as a persistent standalone symptom, denies hopelessness, denies any thoughts of self-harm. This should read as **fatigue-associated low mood, not a primary depressive episode** -- a good candidate should screen for depression explicitly rather than assuming either way.
- **Red flag screening (if asked):**
  - No unintentional weight loss (she's actually gained weight, which is itself a clue).
  - No night sweats.
  - No fevers.
  - No lymphadenopathy noticed.
  - No easy bruising or bleeding beyond heavier periods.
- **PMH:** No major prior medical history. No prior thyroid testing.
- **Medications:** None regularly, occasional ibuprofen for menstrual cramps.
- **Allergies:** NKDA.
- **Family history:** Mother has hypothyroidism. Grandmother had "some kind of thyroid problem" too.
- **Social history:** No smoking, minimal alcohol, no drug use. Works rotating night/day shifts as a nurse (relevant confounder for sleep quality). Married, supportive home situation, denies significant new stressors.
- **ROS:** Denies chest pain, denies palpitations, denies joint pain/swelling, denies rashes, denies GI bleeding.

### Vitals (give on request)
BP 118/76, HR 58 (relatively low, consistent with hypothyroidism), RR 14, Temp 97.6°F (mildly low), O2 sat 99% RA, BMI 27 (up slightly from her baseline per her report).

### Physical exam findings (reveal only for specific system/maneuver requested)
- **General:** Mildly puffy-appearing face, flat affect, slowed speech cadence.
- **Skin:** Dry, cool to touch.
- **Neck/thyroid palpation:** Mildly enlarged, firm, non-tender thyroid gland (goiter), no discrete nodules palpated.
- **Cardiac auscultation:** Regular rate and rhythm, bradycardic, no murmurs.
- **Lung auscultation:** Clear bilaterally.
- **Neuro exam:** Reflexes show delayed relaxation phase (classic "hung-up" reflex), most easily elicited at the Achilles tendon.
- **Lymph node exam:** No cervical, axillary, or other lymphadenopathy.

### Labs/imaging (give only if ordered)
- **TSH:** Elevated at 12.4 mIU/L (high, consistent with primary hypothyroidism).
- **Free T4:** Low.
- **TPO antibodies (if ordered):** Positive, supporting Hashimoto's thyroiditis as the underlying cause.
- **CBC:** Mild microcytic anemia (Hgb 10.8), consistent with her heavier menstrual bleeding -- a real but secondary finding, not the primary driver of her fatigue.
- **Ferritin (if ordered):** Low-normal, supports early/mild iron deficiency from menstrual blood loss.
- **PHQ-9 (if administered):** Mild elevation, below threshold for major depressive disorder, consistent with fatigue-associated low mood rather than a primary depressive episode.

### Model answer for debrief
This picture is most consistent with primary hypothyroidism, likely autoimmune (Hashimoto's) given the family history, supported by cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, bradycardia, a palpable goiter, and delayed reflex relaxation on exam. Iron-deficiency anemia from heavy menstrual bleeding is a real secondary contributor and should be worked up in parallel, not dismissed as incidental. Depression should be explicitly screened rather than assumed either way -- some overlapping symptoms are present, but the pattern (with weight gain rather than loss, cold intolerance, and a goiter) points toward an organic cause driving the mood symptoms rather than a primary depressive episode. Plan: TSH/free T4 (and TPO antibodies to confirm autoimmune etiology), CBC and ferritin for the anemia workup, a formal depression screen, initiate levothyroxine once hypothyroidism is confirmed, address the heavy menstrual bleeding, and set a follow-up interval to recheck TSH and reassess symptoms.
